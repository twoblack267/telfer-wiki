People / Jean Telfer

Jean Telfer

1840 – 1892
Leased/worked farmland at Arden Vale (near Quorn, SA). Later worked properties around Peterborough.
JT

Biography

Third daughter of Francis Telfer (1809–1895) and Jean Murray. After her mother's death, she was step-raised by Dinah Flavel (her father's second wife).

Vital Information

  • Born: 7 August 1840
  • Birth name: Jean Telfer (marriage certificate gives name as "Jane")
  • Died: 15 July 1892, Queens Own Town (Finniss Point), South Australia — heart disease
  • Buried: Strathalbyn Cemetery, South Australia

Marriage (1): Richard Paynter (1838–1869)

  • Marriage date/location: Solemnized by John Kither (Officiating Minister) at Chain of Ponds Parsonage. Witnessed by her father Francis Telfer and Henry Whiting of Chain of Ponds. Jean was several months pregnant at the time.
  • Richard Paynter: Son of Richard Paynter (from Calstock, Cornwall, died Jan 1856 aged 39 after "protracted affliction")
  • Richard's occupation: Initially a shepherd at Koppio (working for Jean's uncle James Telfer), later a school teacher at Saint Kitts (between Kapunda and Truro) — change due to poor health
  • Richard died: 7 February 1869, St. Kitts Creek, of pleurisy, aged 30. Newspaper: "Mr Paynter, the Schoolmaster at St. Kitts Creek, died somewhat suddenly on Sunday last, by which a widow and five children have been left unprovided for."

Family Stories

Left a Widow at 29

When Jean's first husband Richard Paynter died of pleurisy in February 1869 at just 30, he left her with five children and no provision. The local paper put it bluntly — "a widow and five children have been left unprovided for." Jean was pregnant with her fifth child Dinah Flavel Paynter, born five months after Richard died. A schoolteacher who'd been in poor health for some time, Richard's passing left the family scrambling.

(From the Borders to the Bush, pp.76-79)

A Christmas Day Wedding

On Christmas Day 1871, Jean — now going by Jane — married Frederick Parker at Kapunda. Frederick was a bullock driver and itinerant farm labourer, and the marriage took Jean and her children from the relative comfort of Finniss Point to the far north of South Australia around Hawker and Oraparinna. Life in the north was hard and isolated.

(From the Borders to the Bush, pp.77-79)

The Roof-Top Refuge

Jean's eldest son Thomas Paynter built a stone house with a thatched roof at Arden Vale near Quorn. When he and his wife Elizabeth had to walk five miles into town for supplies, they'd put the children on the roof of the house — safe from the dingoes and wild dogs that roamed the area. Years later, Thomas lost a leg slipping under a train and died shortly after at 86. The ruins of that stone house still stand among the peppertrees planted in those early days.

(From the Borders to the Bush, pp.79-81)

The Convict Who Built Lighthouses

Jean's daughter Mary Jane Paynter married John Watson, whose grandfather Charles Watson arrived in Tasmania as a convict on the Asia II in 1827. Charles was an engineer assigned to Public Works, became Engineer in Charge of building lighthouses around Tasmania — Bruny Island, Swan Island, Goose Island, and Tow Head — and even built the causeway at Bridgewater. He got an unconditional pardon in 1834 after serving just 7 of his 14-year sentence. John himself ran away to sea at 12, served on whaling ships, and was a "lime burner" of Arkaba when he married Mary Jane.

(From the Borders to the Bush, pp.78-84)

End of the Line at Finniss

Jean died of heart disease in 1892 at Queens Own Town (Finniss Point), aged just 51, and was buried at Strathalbyn Cemetery. Her husband Frederick outlived her by 36 years, spending his final days living with his daughter Margaret and son-in-law at Wildes Meadow, New South Wales. He was buried at Bowral in 1928.

(From the Borders to the Bush, pp.78-84)|
